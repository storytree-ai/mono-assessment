## Part 1 of 7

monō Forward Deployed Engineer — Candidate Assessment Task. Version 1.1. Audience: Candidate. Format: Two-hour individual work sample followed by a 60-minute working session. Scenario: Fictional; it is not current monō or client work. 1. Purpose. Forward Deployed Engineers at monō work inside a client's real operating environment. They turn ambiguous problems into production systems, own the technical path to adoption and measurable value, and convert what they learn into reusable platform capability. This exercise is designed to let you demonstrate how you: discover and frame the real problem; choose a narrow, valuable first release; build a working technical slice; make sound AI, data, security and production trade-offs; communicate with technical and non-technical stakeholders; respond when facts change; and leave behind reusable capability rather than a one-off demo. We do not expect a production-complete system. We value prioritisation, working evidence and clear judgement over polish or volume. 2. Time and Format. Part A — Individual work sample. Hard timebox: 2 hours. Location: Your choice. Tools: Any development, data-generation and AI tools you normally use. Output: A small working prototype, the supporting artefacts needed to run and demonstrate it, and a concise deployment brief.

## Part 2 of 7

You are responsible for creating or generating the artefacts you need to design, implement and play back your chosen solution—for example, synthetic data, policy rules, documents, emails, API mocks, schemas, prompts, tests or a lightweight interface. There is no prescribed starter project, dataset, technology stack or asset format. Please stop at two hours, even if the work is incomplete. State what you prioritised, what remains and what you would do next. Respecting the timebox is part of the exercise. Part B — Live working session. Duration: 60 minutes. Format: Collaborative review with monō engineering and delivery interviewers. Content: Demonstration, technical discussion, a changed-facts scenario and a short client conversation. The live session is a discussion, not a presentation contest. We will ask questions and may work through changes with you. 3. The Scenario. You have joined a monō pod working with HarbourLine Finance, a fictional Australian and New Zealand equipment lender with approximately 800 employees. HarbourLine has asked the pod to improve its loan-application exception triage process. The current process is slow, inconsistent and dependent on experienced operations analysts. Current workflow. For each application flagged for manual review, an analyst must: 1. open the application in a Salesforce-like CRM; 2. review identity, income, asset and credit documents stored in SharePoint; 3. check broker emails for explanations or missing information; 4. compare the evidence with lending-policy rules; 5. identify missing, contradictory or expired evidence; 6. prepare a concise exception brief for a credit officer; and 7. record the credit officer's decision and rationale. The system must support the analyst and credit officer. It must not autonomously approve or decline credit.

## Part 3 of 7

Baseline (Measure: Current state): Applications requiring manual triage: 240 per business day. Average analyst preparation time: 32 minutes per application. Rework caused by missed or incorrect evidence: 7.5%. Backlog: Approximately 3 business days. Analyst weekly adoption of the current internal tool: 38%. Desired six-week Lighthouse outcome. HarbourLine wants a controlled first release that can demonstrate: analyst preparation time below 10 minutes per application; at least 95% accuracy on mandatory evidence extraction against an agreed test set; zero unapproved production writes; full traceability from source evidence to every generated statement; at least 70% weekly adoption among the pilot analyst group; and a defensible estimate of annual business value. The executive sponsor wants to see a credible working slice in 10 business days. 4. Candidate-Created Assets and Enterprise Constraints. No starter pack is provided. You are free to engineer or generate the assets necessary to design, implement and demonstrate your solution. These may include: synthetic application records in any practical format; fictional document text and broker-email extracts; a compact lending-policy or mandatory-evidence rule set; mocked or stubbed CRM and document-retrieval interfaces; model prompts, wrappers or deterministic substitutes; schemas, evaluation cases, tests and audit records; and a command-line, API or minimal user interface for playback. Create only what is necessary to make your decisions and working slice understandable. Breadth or production-level realism is not expected within the timebox. You may simplify the scenario, provided you state the simplification and preserve the important workflow, safety and decision-making constraints.

## Part 4 of 7

All sample content must be fictional or synthetic. Document material assumptions, make the submission reproducible, and distinguish clearly between an implemented capability, a mock and a proposed production component. Enterprise constraints: Personally identifiable information must remain within HarbourLine's Australian tenant. Raw customer data must not be sent to an unapproved external model endpoint. The initial release is read-only. Any future write to a system of record requires explicit human approval. Every generated claim must be traceable to source evidence or marked as uncertain. The solution must degrade safely if a source system or model is unavailable. Access to one source system may be delayed by up to a week. HarbourLine has one engineer available for approximately four hours per week. The pilot users are experienced analysts who are sceptical because two previous automation pilots created extra work. 5. Your Task. Create the narrowest useful technical and delivery slice you would be prepared to put in front of HarbourLine's pilot analysts after the first 10 business days. Deliverable 1 — Problem and release brief. In the README, cover the following in no more than 500 words: 1. Outcome: What business and user outcome are you targeting? 2. Discovery: What are the five most important questions you would ask before committing to the release? 3. Scope: What is in the first release, and what is explicitly out? 4. Assumptions: What have you assumed because the case is incomplete? 5. Success: What evidence would allow you and the client to say the first release is working?

## Part 5 of 7

Deliverable 2 — Working prototype. Using assets you create or generate, build a small working slice that: ingests one or more fictional synthetic applications in a format you choose; checks the available evidence against a policy or mandatory-evidence rule set you define; identifies missing, expired or contradictory evidence; produces a concise analyst-facing exception brief; cites the source for each material statement or marks uncertainty; records enough structured information to support an audit trail; and handles at least one failure or malformed-input case safely. Your solution may combine deterministic logic and an LLM. We are interested in why you use each, not in maximising model usage. Include the inputs, rules, mocks, prompts, configuration and other assets needed for us to run or understand the slice. Clearly label generated data and simulated integrations. Include at least two meaningful tests or executable checks. A sophisticated interface is not required; a command-line tool, API endpoint or minimal UI is acceptable. Deliverable 3 — Production and evaluation path. Add a one-page maximum production note or diagram covering: target architecture and data flow; identity, access, privacy and audit controls; evaluation dataset and acceptance criteria; model/system failure modes and safe fallback; pilot, human-review and rollout sequence; monitoring, incident response and rollback; analyst adoption and feedback loop; and what you would build, buy or reuse rather than custom-build. Deliverable 4 — Client and product communication. Include both: 1. a client update of 150 words or fewer for HarbourLine's COO explaining what is ready, what is not, the principal risk and the next decision required; and 2. three bullets describing what should be harvested from this deployment into a reusable monō pattern, component or platform improvement.

## Part 6 of 7

6. Submission: a Git repository or ZIP file containing your prototype; all self-created or generated assets required to run and play back the solution; a README with setup, run and demonstration instructions; your problem/release brief; your production and evaluation note; and your client update and harvest bullets. The submission should run locally or provide a practical recorded or guided playback if a dependency cannot be packaged. Include any sample data, rules, prompts, mocks, configuration and test commands needed to reproduce the demonstrated path. Do not use confidential information from any current or former employer or client. 7. Use of AI Tools. AI use is permitted and expected. Use AI as you would in the role. In the README, briefly disclose: the tools/models used; what you delegated to them; the most important output you rejected or corrected; and how you verified the submitted work. You remain accountable for every design choice, line of code and statement. During the live session, you must be able to explain and modify the work without relying on an opaque generated answer. If a paid tool or model would create an unfair cost, tell us. monō will provide access or agree an equivalent route.

## Part 7 of 7

8. Live Working Session: Demonstrate the slice and state your priorities, 10 min (outcome, scope, working evidence); Code and architecture discussion, 20 min (design choices, quality, evaluation, operability); Changed-facts scenario, 15 min (adaptation, trade-offs, sequencing); Client conversation, 10 min (clarity, trust, expectation management); Your questions, 5 min. One standardised change will be introduced, e.g.: the client's privacy lead prohibits the proposed model endpoint; pilot accuracy reaches only 88%; the sponsor asks for automatic CRM writes next week; the document API will not be available for ten business days; or a material error is found after analysts have started using the output. You will revise the plan and explain it to a client stakeholder; not expected to predict the twist. 9. What We Assess: workflow discovery and problem framing; hands-on engineering and technical judgement; applied AI and evaluation discipline; production, security and operational ownership; client communication and embedded judgement; measurable outcome, adoption and expansion thinking; reusability and field-to-platform learning. No reward for unnecessary hours, visual polish, a particular framework or a single correct architecture. 10. Strong work: narrows the problem before building; makes assumptions visible; places deterministic controls around probabilistic components; distinguishes a demo from a safe production path; uses tests and evaluation evidence rather than confidence statements; preserves human decision rights for material credit decisions; communicates bad news early and plainly; measures adoption as well as technical quality; turns a customer-specific lesson into something the next deployment can reuse. Good judgement may result in an incomplete prototype; preferable to a polished solution that ignores the operating reality.
